Mallow's Little While

Through the brook's reflection, and under the willow: a low, round room of roots, at dusk. Through its leafy ceiling you can still see the stepping stones you came by. Nothing here is in a hurry.

You have sat with Mallow before. The door was never locked — and for you, it stays open.

Beneath the willow, at dusk

Mallow “You can sit down while I think.”

She has been out back, where it is quiet — not lost, and never left out on purpose. The rhyme simply finished before her thinking did. There is no puzzle here. There is only a little while, and it is yours too.

🍐 Back at the feast — the long table, the bread basket, the pear cut into thin moons — the sixth place is still set, and now you know whose it is. It was never an empty chair. It was room, left on purpose: the napkin's hem says “Leave a little room,” and the room is left for whoever is still thinking, whenever they are ready to come in. Mallow cuts the pears thin enough for everyone to have another piece — including, now, you.

“I liked walking with you,” Mallow says at last, to the room in general. And when the light goes, the narration finds you both:

And home they went, with room between them for the evening.

On the keepsake's reverse: Mallow's willow room, and a seventh cup, left for the reader.
“For whenever you have a little while.”
